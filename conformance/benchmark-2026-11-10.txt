GPKit at constellation scale: propagation from 10,000 to 40,000 element sets, and what a program does with 10,000 of
them once propagated (looks, map positions, a day's passes over one place, the close-approach screen), with the most
memory held.
Run on 2026-11-10 (UTC), 16:05:58Z to 16:09:21Z, on a Mac17,4 (Apple M5, 10 cores: 4 performance, 6 efficiency,
16 GB), macOS 27.2.1 (26B131), Apple Swift version 6.4.2, release build, GPKit 1.3.0 (main 1f5405b) with this
branch's tool. Each run was started only when the Mac's one-minute load was 12 or less, with no xcodebuild and no
simulator round running. The load at each run's start and end is printed. The Mac was not otherwise idle.

How it was run, the same on any machine:

  swift run -c release --package-path tools/benchmark benchmark --workloads
  swift run -c release --package-path tools/benchmark benchmark --count 20000
  swift run -c release --package-path tools/benchmark benchmark --count 40000

The element sets are made up by the tool's rule (four shells like a large constellation's), as in
benchmark-2026-11-07.txt. No provider's data is in them. Each figure is the median of 5 runs after one run to warm
up, except the close-approach screen, which compares every pair and is timed once. Before anything is timed, the tool
checks that the batch gives the one-at-a-time results, and the spread workloads give the one-at-a-time results, to
the bit. A planted fault in the spread's order stopped it there ("the spread looks are not one at a time's", exit 1).

What it shows:

- Propagation grows with the count. A day at one-minute steps through the batch is 29, 31 and 38 ns a state at
  10,000, 20,000 and 40,000 sets; one at a time it is 149, 161 and 166 ns. 10,000 sets agree with
  benchmark-2026-11-07.txt to within 12 per cent (the batch's day: 0.416 s against 0.371 s), at a higher load.
- A day's passes of 10,000 sets over one place: 7.3 s one at a time, 1.4 s spread over the 10 cores, 65,048 passes.
- Looks and map positions of 10,000 sets at one instant take a few milliseconds.
- The close-approach screen compares every pair, so its time grows as the square of the count: 3.4 s for 500 sets
  and 14.2 s for 1,000. 10,000 would be about 100 times 1,000's, roughly 24 minutes **[inferred, not run]**. Its
  height filter passes over no pair here, since the four made-up shells overlap in height. The screen is a rough one
  (CloseApproach's documentation), and at constellation scale it would want a spatial index before anything else.
- Memory: the day's batch keeps every state of every set, so the process held 857 MB at 10,000 sets, 1,752 MB at
  20,000 and 3,371 MB at 40,000. That is a finding for 2.0: a batch that hands each step to the caller and does not
  keep the day would hold a step's states only **[inferred]**.
